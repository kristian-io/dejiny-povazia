## Summary: what makes Sládkovičovo special

1. **Two towns, then one, under a name with no local link.** Hungarian Diószeg ("walnut corner") and the German colony Németdiószeg, settled under Joseph II in 1786–87, were separate municipalities from 1853 until 1943. The merged town was renamed **Sládkovičovo in 1948**, after the poet Andrej Sládkovič. Sládkovič never had any connection with the place. Older inhabitants still call the renaming a "bad joke". The town is the clearest local example of the post-1945 renaming wave (cf. Matúškovo, also 1948). (S039, S043, S041) **C**
2. **A sugar town built by Viennese capital.** Kuffner & Guttmann of Vienna started a sugar factory in 1867. Karl Kuffner arrived in 1869 aged 22 and made it reputedly the most economical sugar factory in the Monarchy. He became Baron Kuffner "de Diószegh" and shaped the town: the manor (1885–86), the Mária mill (1912), the workers' colony, the park, and the family mausoleum (1926). The factory employed over 1,000 people and closed in **2000**. (S040, S043, S049, S050, S052) **C**
3. **Darwin connection.** In 1876 Karl Kuffner of "Dioszegh/Ungarn" was photographed for the album of German and Austrian admirers sent to Charles Darwin. (S050, Commons file from the Darwin Correspondence Project) **L**
4. **One of the region's best-documented ethnic upheavals.** The 1938 Hungarian count gave 4,808 people (84% Hungarian, about 6% German, 9% Slovak, 152 Jews). Then came, in order: deportation of the Jews in 1944, expulsion of about 60 Germans in spring 1946, re-Slovakisation of 2,586 people, forced labour deportations to Bohemia in late 1946, and the population exchange of 1947 (144 Hungarian families listed to leave; 827 Slovaks arrived). By 1948 the town had 4,950 people from at least four origins. (S045, S043, S039) **L/C**
5. **Archaeology at Nové diely (1966–67 road rescue digs).** Finds include one of the best Bell Beaker graves in SW Slovakia, an Early Roman-period Germanic (Quadi-era) site, and a **10th-century Old Hungarian horse-rider grave**. Točík read the rider grave as evidence of an Old Hungarian military frontier facing the young Přemyslid state across the Váh. There are also an 11th-century cemetery and an 11th–12th-century house. (S032, S046, S041) **L**
6. **A market town on the Pressburg–Nitra highway.** Rudolf II granted market privileges in 1582. For about 250 years (c. 1553–1782) the town belonged to the **Poor Clares** (Óbuda/Buda, later Pressburg). It was raided by the Ottomans (1530, 1599, 1673–83), and the neighbouring village of Dudvágszeg was destroyed. Starhemberg's imperial army fortified it in 1707, and the whole town burned in 1709. (S039, S042, S044) **C/L**
7. **Railway town since 1850.** It lies on the first Pressburg–Pest line (1850). The station was in the German part. (S039, S043) **C**

The Roma population was estimated at 681 (12.2%) in the 2013 Atlas, against 51 who declared Roma nationality in 2011 (S040). **Storyline:** about 54% Hungarian / 21% German (1880) → 84% Hungarian (1938) → 54.7% "re-Slovak" (1946) → 25% Hungarian (2021).
